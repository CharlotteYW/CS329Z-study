# Active Paper Reading and Presentation

Use the [course index](../resources/course-materials.md) to select the exact paper/version. Treat blog posts, framework docs, papers, and benchmark implementations as different kinds of evidence. No paper has been assessed yet.

## Reading Protocol

Before reading, predict the problem and why a simpler approach might fail. First inspect the abstract, main mechanism, and one central result. Then examine methods and limitations that affect the claim you want to use. Spend extra time on a relevant experiment rather than generating a long passive summary.

Create an English note when there is a meaningful learner contribution. Include:

1. **Problem:** What concrete task or limitation is addressed?
2. **Motivation:** Why does it matter, and to whom?
3. **Contribution:** What is new relative to the compared alternatives?
4. **Mechanism:** What are the inputs, state, components, outputs, and update/control rules?
5. **Evaluation:** Which tasks, datasets, baselines, metrics, and budgets are used?
6. **Evidence:** Which experiment supports which claim? Record a section/table/figure reference and the version read.
7. **Limitations:** What remains untested or confounded? Distinguish the authors' stated limitations from your own critique.
8. **Comparison:** What alternative could explain the result or offer a simpler solution?
9. **Engineering lesson:** What would you adopt, test, or reject in your own system?

Add one question you cannot yet answer. Summarize the paper orally in three minutes without looking at the abstract. The mentor should challenge a claim or propose a counterexample, then ask you to apply the mechanism to a new setting. If code is useful, inspect the authors' implementation or reproduce one small behavior; do not search for assignment solutions.

For blog posts without experiments, say so. For framework docs, test interface claims against the installed version. Do not invent empirical support where none is provided.

## Paper-Presentation Milestone

The official course includes a paper video; the schedule/logistics specify **10–15 minutes** and link a [handout](https://web.stanford.edu/class/cs329z/assignments/paper_video.pdf). The handout is reachable; its detailed rubric must be read before selecting and recording the final presentation. Use the current handout to resolve requirements, not this planning guide.

Suggested self-study sequence:

1. After M4, shortlist two or three course-relevant papers and explain their learning value.
2. Select one after checking the handout's scope; preserve its version and bibliographic details.
3. Complete the nine prompts above and identify the weakest part of your understanding.
4. Build a learner-authored outline covering problem, mechanism, evidence, limitations, and engineering implications. The mentor can critique the outline and rehearse questions.
5. Rehearse with a timer, explain a figure without reading its caption, and answer a counterargument.
6. Record only after comprehension is demonstrated; review clarity and accuracy against the official rubric.

Self-study completion requires an understandable explanation, an accurate account of evidence and limitations, and answers to follow-up questions. A video file alone does not demonstrate mastery. Track this milestone separately from lecture completion in `PROGRESS.md`.
